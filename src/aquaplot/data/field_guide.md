# How to check a stream

Everything here takes about twenty minutes and needs nothing you cannot buy in a
supermarket. It is written for someone who has never done this before.

## Before you go

**What to bring**

- A phone.
- A pale plastic tray, tub or takeaway container. Pale, so you can see what moves against it.
- A small net if you have one. A kitchen sieve works. So do your hands.
- Wellingtons, or shoes you do not mind soaking.
- A small paintbrush or a teaspoon, for lifting animals without squashing them.

**Safety, which matters more than the data**

- Never go alone, and tell someone where you are going.
- Stay out of water deeper than your knee, and out of any water that is moving
  fast enough to push against your legs.
- Do not go in after heavy rain. Urban streams rise fast, and a sewer overflow
  discharges exactly then.
- Cover any cut with a waterproof plaster before you start, and wash your hands
  before you eat or touch your face. Urban water carries bacteria and, rarely,
  leptospirosis. If you get flu-like symptoms in the week after contact, see a
  doctor and tell them you were in a stream.
- If you can smell sewage, solvent or fuel, or you can see a blue-green bloom or
  an oily film: **do not go in**. Photograph it from the bank, answer the
  questions, and submit. That is a more valuable record than a sample.
- Check whether you need permission. Most public parks are fine; private land
  and drinking-water catchments are not.

**Permission and season**

- Some countries require a licence to sample fish; nobody needs one to look at
  invertebrates. If in doubt, ask your local river trust or environment agency.
- Any time of year works. Spring and early summer hold the most animals, but a
  winter reading at the same spot is just as useful, because what matters is the
  *series* of visits.

## Choosing a spot

Find a **riffle**: a shallow stretch where the water runs fast and broken over
stones and gravel, the noisy bit. This is where the oxygen is and where the
sensitive animals live, so it is the fairest place to judge a stream.

If there is no riffle, take the fastest, shallowest, stoniest place you can reach
safely, and say so in the notes. The most important thing is to **go back to the
same spot every time**, so AquaPlot can join your visits into a trend. It groups
anything within about a hundred metres automatically, and asks you when you are
close to somewhere you have been before.

Avoid sampling right below a bridge, a pipe or a weir unless that is the thing
you are investigating. Those places don't represent the rest of the stream.

## Photograph the stream

Stand on the bank and take one photograph that shows:

- the water,
- both banks if you can, and
- what is growing along the edge.

That single picture answers most of the visual questions: how clear the water is,
its colour, algae, foam, litter, what the banks are made of, how much shade there
is. Take it *before* you get in and stir everything up.

## Take a sample

1. Put a few centimetres of stream water in your tray and set it on the bank.
2. Stand **downstream** of the patch you are going to disturb, so the current
   carries what you dislodge towards you.
3. **Kick sample:** hold the net just downstream of your feet and scuff the
   stream bed with your heel for about thirty seconds, over an area the size of a
   dinner plate. Animals wash off the stones into the net.
   **Without a net:** lift three or four stones the size of your fist, straight
   up, and rinse each one into the tray with your hands.
4. Tip the catch into the tray.
5. **Wait a minute.** The silt settles and the animals start moving, and movement
   is what makes them visible. This is the step everyone skips, and it is the
   difference between a photograph of grit and a photograph of a stream.

## Photograph the tray

- Get close, and hold the phone steady and parallel to the water surface.
- Turn off the flash; use the shade of your own body to kill reflections instead.
- Take several photographs. Focus on the biggest and the most numerous animals.
- Tap to focus on an animal before you shoot. A blurred photograph is an honest
  "unknown" rather than a wrong identification.

**What you are looking for,** roughly best news first:

| What you see | What it is | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Two long tails, flat body, clinging under stones | Stonefly | Cold, clean, well-oxygenated water |
| Three tails, gills along the sides | Mayfly | Clean water; the flat-headed ones are fussiest |
| A grub in a tube of sand, twigs or leaf pieces | Cased caddisfly | Decent condition |
| A bright green grub with no case | Free-living caddisfly | Fast, clean riffles |
| A tiny hard beetle walking slowly on a stone | Riffle beetle | Steady oxygen year-round |
| Flattened side-to-side, curls into a C, scuttles | Freshwater shrimp | Common in fair and middling streams |
| Flattened top-to-bottom, crawls like a woodlouse | Water hoglouse | Tolerant; a warning sign if it dominates |
| Thin bright red worms wriggling in silt | Bloodworm (midge larva) | Very low oxygen |
| Tufts of thin red worms in black mud | Sludge worm | Organic pollution |
| A grub with a long thin tail like a snorkel | Rat-tailed maggot | Almost no oxygen at all |

The identification guide inside the app has all fifty-three families with what to
look for. You do not have to name anything: photograph the tray, and confirm or
correct what the app proposes.

## Put everything back

Tip the tray gently back into the stream, in the shallows, close to where you
took it. Return the stones the same way up. Before you visit another stream,
**check, clean and dry** your tray and boots. Invasive species and diseases
spread on wet equipment more reliably than they spread through the water.

## Answer the two questions only you can answer

**What can you smell?** No camera can. This is the single most informative thing
you will record. Sewage or rotten eggs, or a chemical or solvent smell, changes
the result more than anything else on the page.

**Who gets into this water?** A degraded stream nobody touches is an ecological
problem. A degraded stream children paddle in is also a public-health one, and
the advice you get should be different.

## Then

Submit. If you have no signal, submit anyway: the assessment and the photographs
are saved on your phone and send themselves when you are back in coverage.

Check the handful of things the app asks you to confirm. It matters: the app
holds back any health warning until a person has confirmed the observation it
rests on.

If anything came back at concern or alert level, open the report and send it to
your water authority or environmental regulator. A dated, located, photographed
record from a member of the public is the kind of evidence that gets a sewer
network inspected. A complaint without one usually is not.

**Then go back in a month.** One reading only shows a single day. Readings over
months show whether the stream is getting better or worse, and that is what gets
a municipality to act.

---

*AquaPlot is a screening tool. It is not a Water Framework Directive
classification, and it is not a medical or regulatory determination. Its job is
to tell you what is worth reporting, and to whom.*
